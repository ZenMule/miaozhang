Part I: One sentence, many meanings

Something that actually happened in this room

  • Saturday is a class day for us.
  • It was October 3rd, in the middle of the National Day holiday in mainland China.
  • A Saturday — students at other universities do not have classes on the weekend.
  • Every other classroom in the building was empty.

What I said when I walked in

“Welcome to the lonely Saturday, once again.”

You laughed. Immediately — before I said anything else.

Why?

Question 1: the literal sentence

Forget the laughter for a moment.

  • What does the sentence literally say?
  • What would it mean to someone who heard only the words — no classroom, no October 3rd, no empty building?

Sentence meaning

Welcome to the lonely Saturday, once again.

  • A greeting: the speaker welcomes the hearers.
  • A claim: this Saturday is lonely.
  • A claim about history: this has happened before — once again.

This is the sentence meaning: what the words conventionally say.

But that is not what you received

Nobody laughed at “the Saturday is lonely” as a report about a day.

You heard the words, and in the same instant you arrived at something more — enough to make you laugh.

Question 2: What did you actually understand?

What did I really mean?

The literal sentence is a welcome. But what was I doing with these words?

List every candidate interpretation you can defend:

  • gentle sarcasm about having class during a national holiday;
  • a shared complaint — teacher and students are both here;
  • a joke to warm up the room;
  • something else you heard?

Sentence meaning vs. utterance meaning

  • Pragmatics studies how we use context to make inferences from sentence meaning to utterance meaning (Fasold, 1990; O’Keeffe, Clancy, & Adolphs, 2011, pp. 1–2).
  • An inference is a conclusion drawn from available evidence (Christie, 2000).

Sentence meaning vs. utterance meaning

Question Kind of meaning
What does the sentence conventionally say? Sentence meaning
What might this speaker mean by saying it here? Utterance meaning

Question 3: where did the extra meanings come from?

You never left the literal sentence behind by guessing.

Name the elements of the situation that led you to each interpretation. One clue per sticky note, one interpretation at a time.

Three types of context

The clues you named fall into three groups:

Type What speakers draw on
Situational What they can see around them — the physical setting of the talk
Background knowledge What they know about each other (interpersonal knowledge) and about the world (cultural knowledge)
Co-textual What they have been saying — the surrounding discourse

Pragmatic choices encode position, time, power, status, gender, age — all recoverable from context.

Quick check

Classify each clue:

  • No other classes can be seen at the same time on the 3rd floor of B building.
  • You know the teacher has taught this course before.
  • The first sentence spoken after the greeting.

Part II: Why can we infer at all?

A harder question

Not what context you used —

Why were you able to infer anything in the first place?

Why didn’t you just hear a slightly odd greeting, shrug, and stop there?

Because we are cooperative

Grice (1975: 45): conversations are

“to some degree at least, cooperative efforts; and each participant recognises in them, to some extent, a common purpose or set of purposes, or at least a mutually accepted direction.”

The Cooperative Principle: we assume each contribution fits the current purpose of the exchange — so when the literal content does not fit, we search for an interpretation that does.

Cooperation at work on one sentence

Welcome to the lonely Saturday, once again.

A Saturday is not literally lonely.

  • Because we assume the contribution fits;
  • We start searching the context for clues for interpretation;
  • The result: “He is joking about our shared situation” — an implicature, not a stated meaning.

Cooperation ≠ being nice or truthful

The Cooperative Principle is not a claim that speakers are honest, kind, or agreeable.

It is a working assumption that talk is purposeful: that there is a point to find.

That assumption is what licenses the search for implied meaning.

Four expectations: Grice’s maxims

Maxim Ask…
Quantity Is enough information given for this purpose, without unnecessary detail?
Quality Is the speaker presenting something they believe and have evidence for?
Relation How does this contribution connect to what matters now?
Manner Is it clear, brief, and orderly?

When a maxim seems unmet

Treat a mismatch as a question, not a verdict.

  • An obvious, deliberate flout may invite an implicature — as with lonely Saturday.
  • A quiet violation may mislead.
  • Two expectations may clash.

One mismatch alone does not reveal a speaker’s intention.

The maxims at work: ordering coffee

An ordinary exchange at a chained Café like Starbucks.

Staff: Hi, what can I get for you?
Customer: A grande latte, please.
Staff: Hot or iced?
Customer: Iced.
Staff: “For here or to go?”
Customer: “To go.”
Staff: “Anything else?”
Customer: “No, that’s all.”
Staff: “Okay — a grande latte to go. That’ll be 7.90.”

Debrief: the maxims nobody noticed

The exchange ran smoothly because every turn was:

  • Quantity — enough information for the purpose, no more: a grande latte;
  • Quality — real order, real price; both sides act on what they believe;
  • Relation — every turn serves the shared purpose: getting a drink ordered;
  • Manner — shop shorthand, clear steps, orderly closing summary.

We only notice the maxims when someone breaks one.

However, what if it’s a different kind of Café?

What if it’s a very small family-owned Café that serves only one type of coffee (e.g., espresso)?

What would have been different while ordering?

Part III: Applying the machinery

A three-step analysis frame

  1. Describe the utterance without interpreting it.
  2. Identify contextual clues — situational, background, co-textual.
  3. Propose an interpretation and explain the link.

An inference is a reasonable hypothesis, not mind-reading.

The missing premise

A: Can you bring a USB?
B: I’ll take my laptop.

  1. What does B literally say?
  2. What answer might A infer?
  3. What assumption connects B’s statement to that answer?
  4. Invent a context in which the inference would be wrong.

Context detectives: a new scene

A: Do you mind if I leave the window open?
B: It’s cold in here.

  1. Classify the clues as situational, background knowledge, or co-textual.
  2. What might B mean? One alternative per reading, with evidence.
  3. Which maxim supports your preferred reading? Which clue complicates it?

Build a mini-dialogue

Write a three-turn dialogue containing one utterance that could have at least two interpretations.

Include:

  • the shared words;
  • two different possible contexts;
  • one clue favoring each interpretation.

Exchange and interpret

Give your dialogue to another group without explaining your intention.

They write:

  1. one likely interpretation;
  2. the clue they used — situational, background, or co-textual;
  3. one alternative interpretation.

Then compare their reading with your intention.

Inference or judgment?

A: Can you join our rehearsal tonight?
B: I have work due tomorrow.

Discuss the claim: “B doesn’t care about the group.”

  1. What does B say, and what is inferred?
  2. Give two possible readings with evidence.
  3. Rewrite the claim cautiously; suggest a neutral follow-up question.

Exit check

Choose It’s noisy in here or Do you have a pen?

Give one context where the utterance may do more than its literal form suggests. Identify one clue and name its context type. Explain the inference in one or two sentences, using cautious language.
