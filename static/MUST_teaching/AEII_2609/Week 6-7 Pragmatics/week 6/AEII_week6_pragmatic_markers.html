What is a pragmatic marker?

Aijmer (2015) uses pragmatic marker as an umbrella term for a large, messy family of expressions: well, you know, I mean, I think, oh, actually, sort of, innit, like…

  • Terminology is unsettled — “discursive marker”, “discourse particle”, and “discourse marker” are used differently by different scholars.
  • Following Fraser (1996): a discourse marker (narrow sense) is the subtype that signals how the basic message relates to the foregoing discourse.

Discussion: is “umbrella term” honest, or a problem for a science of language?

The data first: what markers do

Two real examples from the reading (Aijmer 2015):

“He’s rich I mean, (really) rich.” — invites the hearer to infer added information.

“I think I might get us sort of a set for all of us.” — a suggestion, softened by a whole cluster of hedges.

  • What job are I mean / I think doing here — message content, or something else?

Defining features

Aijmer (2015, pp. 199–200) collects the standard criteria:

  • Bracket units of talk — sequentially dependent (Schiffrin 1987, from eleven items: oh, well, and, but, or, so, because, now, then, I mean, you know).
  • Weakened lexical meaning — the category is “in constant flux”, recruiting new members.
  • Outside propositional content — optional “inserts”, occurring initially, medially, or finally.
  • Procedural rather than conceptual — tools for guiding interpretation, not for describing the world.

Prototypicality

Not all markers are equally marker-like.

  • Interjections (oh, ah) are less prototypical: not fully grammaticalized, with a “dual identity” — but they can open turns, fill pauses, signal attention.
  • Comment clauses (I think, I don’t know) sit near the boundary — they arguably still encode conceptual content (Ifantidou 1994).

This is a good moment to ask students why a category might have fuzzy edges — markers grammaticalize, so today’s adverb is tomorrow’s marker.

Two big functional families

Following Halliday’s distinction, pragmatic markers serve:

Function What the marker does Examples from the paper
Interpersonal hedge or soften, express solidarity, signal stance I think as a politeness hedge; well softening disagreement
Textual attract attention, signal frames or boundaries, topic shift now, right, OK opening or closing a move

Östman (1995) adds a three-parameter view: coherence, politeness, involvement.

Why corpus linguistics?

  • PMs have a “symbiotic link” with conversation — they barely appear in writing.
  • Spoken corpora made them visible and countable: London–Lund, COLT (London teenagers: innit, like), ICE-GB.
  • Findings are genre-sensitive: well in lawyers’ cross-examinations heightens the challenge rather than softening (Hale 1999); I think is more frequent in political debates than in casual conversation.

Same marker, different job — depending on where it lives.

Case study: I think

Aijmer’s own study, two genres, 100 examples each:

  • Frequency: 241.5 per 100k words in speech vs. 17.2 in writing; broadcast discussions highest of all text types.
  • Conversation: usually initial (77%); core function = subjective opinion; extended into hedging clusters (might, sort of, I don’t know) and hesitation fillers.
  • Broadcast discussion: initial and strategic (86%) — introduces lengthy opinions, claims authority and objectivity, often chains with and.

Core meaning + a flexible “meaning potential” extended by context.

What about learners?

Aijmer (2015, p. 204):

“Learners both over-use and under-use pragmatic markers in comparison with native speakers.”

  • Learner-corpus studies (Hasselgren 2002; Müller 2005; Gilquin 2008; Aijmer 2011).
  • Gilquin’s question: “pragmatic deficiency or difference?”
  • ELF speakers stretch markers in unusual ways that still “fulfil the speakers’ needs”.

Discussion 1: the definition problem

  1. Take Aijmer’s criteria (bracketing talk, weakened meaning, outside proposition, procedural meaning). Test them against one marker of your choice — does it pass all four?
  2. Where do the criteria break? Report one counterexample to the group.
  3. Conclude: is “pragmatic marker” a natural kind, a convenient basket, or a stage in grammaticalization?

Discussion 2: form, function, and the learner

  1. From the I think case study: why does the same marker signal modesty in conversation but authority on a political talk show? What does this say about “the meaning” of a marker?
  2. Apply this to your own English: which markers do you (or classmates) over-use or under-use? Deficiency or difference?

Wrap-up: three things to keep

  • Pragmatic markers are defined by function, not by word class — a fuzzy, changing category.
  • One form, many functions — core meaning + meaning potential, shaped by genre and situation.
  • For learners, markers are a pragmatics problem, not a vocabulary problem — and corpora are how we can see it.

Questions for the essay

Which everyday conversation this week could you record (ethically) and mine for one marker? What would you count — position, co-occurring hedges, genre?
