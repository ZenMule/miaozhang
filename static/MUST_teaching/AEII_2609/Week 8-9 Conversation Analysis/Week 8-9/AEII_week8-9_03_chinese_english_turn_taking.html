This deck assumes the TCU/TRP, sequence, repair, and evidence foundations from Decks 1–2; do not reteach those definitions. Unless specified, dialogues are instructor-created, including translations. They illustrate questions, not observed language-wide differences. Objectives: compare language-specific resources and study-bounded findings; distinguish translation from analysis; critique an overgeneralized comparison. Evidence: students explain a paired example, propose a contrast case, and limit a claim from a research dataset. Allow protected thinking before each explanation. “Chinese” here means Mandarin; the comparative study concerns American English, not all English varieties or all Chinese languages.

Could some interactional work be conveyed through delivery rather than an extra word?

A different resource—not a different system

Mandarin final particles are one language-specific resource for turn design.

English responses also use wording, prosody, and sequence to convey how an answer is offered.

A translation can preserve an answer while losing some of its delivery or stance.

We must analyze the original-language turn.

Li (2014), pp. 60–69, on completion-relevant final particles; Wu (2016a), pp. 194–200, on a-suffixed answers. Do not assign the isolated teaching example a fixed function such as politeness, obviousness, or impatience. The English example is a demonstration possibility, not a matched research case.

Wu initially linked final a in answers to an epistemic gap: one participant knowing what another did not.

But answers without a also involved that gap.

The first account did not explain the contrast.

What should an analyst compare next?

Wu (2016a), pp. 196–200. Epistemic gap is glossed only to explain the rejected account; do not teach it as the established function of a. Her method illustrates why Mandarin particles require contrastive collections, not a dictionary gloss or one convenient English translation.

Topic-comment is a resource, not a stereotype

In topic-comment construction, a topic is introduced and a comment says something about it.

Li’s Mandarin recipients often wait for the comment despite a boundary after the topic.

English can also introduce a topic separately.

The question is how particular constructions organize entry—not whether only Chinese speakers use topics.

Li (2014), pp. 36–44, 69–70. English example is instructor-created, not Li’s comparative evidence. Avoid claiming all topic-comment boundaries suppress speaker change. Li’s empirical account concerns her local Mandarin sequences.

Where does the action become clear?

A: 我把那本书……还给小王了。
   I [那本书 = that book] ... returned it to Xiao Wang.
A: I returned ... that book to Xiao Wang.

When do you first know that A is describing a return?

Which wording places that clue earlier?

Teaching translations expose order, not natural word-for-word English. The Mandarin example uses a ba construction with preverbal object; do not call all Mandarin SOV or claim every English verb appears early. Li (2014), pp. 54–60, 69–70, contrasts ordering and its projective consequences.

Mandarin offers different ordering possibilities

Mandarin has constructions with objects before or after the verb.

Li shows that ordering can affect how early an action and its possible ending become recognizable.

The two examples do not prove a whole language is less predictable.

What construction—not just which language—would you compare?

Tone language, but still turn-projecting prosody

Does a rising voice mean the same thing?

Mandarin 来 (lái, “come”) has a lexical rising tone.

English “Come?” may have a rising contour that helps shape the utterance.

Does a rising Mandarin syllable automatically signal a question?

Could Mandarin speakers use intonation as well as lexical tone?

Lexical tone distinguishes lexical items; intonation organizes larger stretches and interaction. Do not equate English rise with every question or imply Mandarin lacks intonation. Li (2014), ch. 4, esp. pp. 137–138, 241–242.

Compare the full contour, not one syllable

Mandarin lexical tone and turn-level prosody coexist.

Li identifies a possible last accent followed by low pitch movement in some Mandarin turn completions.

Turn-projecting accent has also been investigated in English.

Do lexical tones remove the need to listen to the larger turn?

Li (2014), pp. 110–137, 241–242; her review of English phonetic studies, pp. 12–15. This is shared resource use despite a phonological difference, not a direct measured comparison or a fixed completion formula.

Where does one intonation unit end?

Listen to one short turn from a Mandarin recording.

Where does a coherent stretch of pitch seem to end?

Does a pause alone tell you where the boundary is?

Use a consented class recording when available. Li (2014), pp. 71–83, defines an intonation unit (IU) as a stretch produced under a single coherent contour. Auditory clues can include a pause, pitch reset, final-syllable lengthening, or anacrusis; no single clue is a mechanical boundary detector. Do not treat punctuation as acoustic evidence.

An intonation unit is not automatically a turn

An intonation unit (IU) is a prosodic unit—not necessarily a clause, a TCU, or a complete turn.

One TCU may unfold across more than one IU; an IU boundary does not by itself prove that a speaker change is due.

What does the next speaker actually do?

Li (2014), pp. 83–104, examines the relationship between intonation units, TCUs, and turns. Keep prosodic segmentation separate from sequential claims: use the surrounding talk to establish whether a boundary is treated as a place for speaker change.

One Mandarin expression, more than one direction

Is 哎呦 responding to news?

A: 他住院了。
   He has been hospitalized.
B: 哎呦，怎么回事？
   Aiyou, what happened?

Which prior event makes B’s response understandable?

Is B reacting to something already mentioned?

Instructor-created scenario. Accept concern, alarm, or a display of unexpectedness as candidates; do not infer a private emotion with certainty. Wu (2016b), pp. 212–216, provides the research basis for backward-looking usage.

Or preparing us for what’s coming?

A: 说起那家店……
   Speaking of that restaurant...
A: 哎呦，他们一杯咖啡卖一百块！
   Aiyou, they charge a hundred for one coffee!

What matter follows 哎呦?

Does it frame information already given—or information about to be delivered?

Instructor-created example based on the directional distinction, not a quotation or empirical observation from Wu. Demonstrate aiyou joined prosodically to the following talk; contrast with a detached delivery without making it a rigid rule.

Can “oh” solve the translation?

A: He has been hospitalized.
B: Oh, what happened?
A: Speaking of that restaurant...
A: Oh, they charge a hundred for one coffee!

Which English delivery would fit each?

Does choosing “oh” preserve everything about the Mandarin turn?

Position and prosody—not a one-word equivalent

Wu distinguishes backward-looking and forward-looking aiyou-prefacing.

Its prosodic attachment to following talk can help show directionality.

English translations such as “oh” need their own sequential analysis.

Do not assume a one-to-one equivalent or that only Mandarin marks stance.

Wu (2016b), pp. 216–231: backward-looking aiyou often forms an independent TCU; forward-looking aiyou is commonly latched prosodically to the following TCU. Keep this a tendency, not a deterministic pronunciation rule. This lesson does not present “oh” as Wu’s tested English counterpart.

What limits a language-only explanation?

Zhu and Boxer (2021), pp. 234–236. Their qualitative method is interactional sociolinguistics, not a controlled experiment or identical matched-task CA corpus. Do not represent the total dataset durations as equal numbers of opportunities.

Which differences occurred in those recordings?

Both datasets included brief overlap and weak disagreement.

Extended concurrent speech and strong disagreement occurred in the Mandarin data; the researchers did not locate them in the English data.

Does “not located in these recordings” mean “impossible in English”?

Zhu and Boxer (2021), pp. 236–249. Extended concurrent speech is their term for prolonged simultaneous speaking, operationalized with onset more than three words before completion; Chinese words are not characters. No prevalence rate or significance test is supplied here.

Did the Mandarin participants call it rude?

Some Mandarin participants watched clips individually and discussed the behavior.

They did not regard the reported extended concurrent speech and strong disagreement as abnormal.

How might equal status, familiar company, low stakes, and relevance matter?

Zhu and Boxer (2021), pp. 236, 248–249. This is contextual appropriateness, not proof that every participant liked every event or that all Chinese speakers prefer overlap. The study did not conduct equivalent English interviews because the target practices were not located there.

Which sounds unfriendly? Can the text alone settle it?

A finding is not a national personality

Zhu and Boxer’s Mandarin examples show strong disagreement that can be understood as sincerity or solidarity.

Delivery, relationships, and local context shape its reception.

The finding challenges an automatic link between direct disagreement and hostility.

It does not establish “Chinese people are direct; English speakers are indirect.”

Zhu and Boxer (2021), pp. 247–249: flat tone, low pitch, and soft volume may mitigate the reported strong disagreement. Their English data lacked this practice; the teaching pair does not contradict that study-bounded finding.

What changes when we do CA in another language?

Different language, different method?

Wu analyzes Mandarin through collections, sequential position, and participants’ displayed understandings.

Those CA questions do not become a separate “Chinese method.”

What changes is the resource being examined: a, aiyou, a construction, or prosody in its original-language context.

Could an English translation hide the contrast we need to explain?

Wu (2016a), pp. 179–200. Differentiate her CA methods from Zhu and Boxer’s interactional sociolinguistics and follow-up interviews. Interviews provide reported perceptions; they do not replace next-turn evidence in a CA analysis.

Same toolkit—careful transcription

Li uses GAT 2; our class toolkit uses Jefferson-style conventions.

Both can preserve timing, overlap, and delivery, but some symbols differ.

Keep the Mandarin original, explain glosses, and align visible conduct where available.

Would comparing Mandarin video with English audio tell us which language uses more gesture?

Li (2014), transcription conventions pp. ix–x and ch. 2. In GAT 2 a dash can mark level final pitch; our class dash marks cut-off. Consult each reading’s legend. Li studies Mandarin multimodality; none of these sources proves a quantitative Chinese–English gesture difference.

Which conclusions survive?

  • “Mandarin particles deserve analysis beyond their English translations.”
  • “Every Mandarin particle ends a turn.”
  • “Extended concurrent speech occurred in this Mandarin dataset.”
  • “English speakers never overlap for long.”

Which are supported? Which go beyond the evidence?

Expected: first and third are defensible with source/context qualification; second and fourth are not. Ask for the precise counterargument, not just labels.

What would a fair comparison need?

Compare the same activity and comparable speaker roles.

Preserve both original languages and equivalent recording detail.

Analyze similar opportunities—not just convenient examples.

Which apparent difference might vanish after those changes?

Apply to the existing bilingual map-task recordings. Account for task order, familiarity, proficiency, and consent. Do not repeat the full methods workshop or create an additional submission requirement.

Different resources; evidence-limited differences

Mandarin particles, constructions, and lexical tone raise specific analytic questions.

Some Chinese–English differences were observed in particular datasets—not in every interaction.

The CA commitment remains: explain the action in its sequence, and show what supports the claim.
