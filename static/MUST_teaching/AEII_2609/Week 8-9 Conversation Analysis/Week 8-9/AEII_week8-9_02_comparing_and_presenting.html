Expected—but not guaranteed

An adjacency pair links two actions, such as question–answer or greeting–greeting.

The first pair part makes a second pair part conditionally relevant: expected, and noticeable if absent.

It does not guarantee that the response will occur.

Assigned chapter, pp. 43–54. Elicit the expectation from the two checkpoint examples before naming it. Do not teach adjacency as simply two adjacent sentences: pair parts may be separated by an insertion sequence.

The next turn is evidence—not mind reading

A next turn displays an understanding of the prior turn.

In one exchange, A confirms information. In the other, A treats B’s response as doubt.

We can analyze those displayed understandings.

We cannot conclude that we know B’s private intention.

Wu (2016a), pp. 193–194, sets out action formulation, grounding in participants’ orientations, and the resources implementing the action. Discuss a possible misunderstanding: a recipient’s interpretation is evidence, not infallible truth.

Don’t turn a gesture into a dictionary entry

A gesture’s recovery or hold can contribute to projecting completion or continuation.

But a held hand might also maintain a referent or position.

Examine the action, speech, and next conduct together.

Li (2014), pp. 157–176, 219–225, 239–243. Gesture recovery often aligns with possible completion in her Mandarin data; gesture holding can be oriented to as continuation. These demonstrations illustrate an analytic problem, not universal body-language meanings or a cross-language difference.

Convergence and divergence

Convergence: resources support the same turn trajectory.

Divergence: syntax, prosody, visible conduct, or action suggest different trajectories.

When cues diverge, participants may interpret them differently.

Follow their conduct instead of deciding that one cue always wins.

Li (2014), pp. 196–240. The preceding exchange illustrates contingency and resumption after an incoming turn; it is not a quotation from Li. Protect time for alternative interpretations before supplying these terms.

Counts need a defined basis

Define the event and the opportunity:

  • pauses per comparable duration;
  • repair cases relative to comparable instructions;
  • overlaps at comparable candidate completions.

Then inspect the sequences. A rate alone cannot explain what the event was doing.

Do not invent results or imply one denominator fits every question. Use comparable activities and explain coding choices. For the earlier illustration, compare repair per instruction only if instruction units and opportunities can be defined consistently; preserve sequential analysis alongside counts.
