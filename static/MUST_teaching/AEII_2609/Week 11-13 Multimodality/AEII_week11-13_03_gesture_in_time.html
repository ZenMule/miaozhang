What casual observers notice

Kendon (2004) points out that what people casually call “a gesture” is usually just the stroke — the most expressive part.

For a proper account of how speech and gesture are organized together, that is not enough.

We need the whole anatomy.

The anatomy of a gesture

Kendon: units and phrases

Term Kendon’s definition
gesture unit the entire excursion from rest back to rest; contains one or more gesture phrases
gesture phrase exactly one stroke, plus any preparation and any post-stroke hold
preparation movement leading up to the stroke
stroke where the gesture’s expression is clearest
post-stroke hold articulator sustained where the stroke ended
recovery the hand relaxes and returns to rest

Two terms that are easy to miss

Nucleus = stroke + any post-stroke hold

  • usually interpreted as the part that carries the gesture’s meaning
  • Kendon notes that stroke and post-stroke hold often bracket a semantically complete stretch of speech

Recovery is part of the gesture unit — but not part of the gesture phrase.

Back to the movement

Use Kendon’s terms:

  • How many gesture units?
  • How many gesture phrases?
  • Where is each stroke? Is there a post-stroke hold?
  • Where does the recovery start?

Return to Kendon’s rice-scattering case: one unit, one phrase, one stroke containing two action phases. Preparation lifts and shapes the hand; recovery returns it to the table. Do not turn each wrist movement into a separate stroke or invent a hold. Kendon (2004), pp. 114–115.

Two wrist actions, one stroke

Part of the rice-scattering action Kendon’s analysis
hand leaves the table and gets ready preparation
repeated scattering movement one stroke, with two action phases
hand returns to the table recovery, outside the phrase

One unit; one phrase. A movement count is not automatically a gesture count.

Kendon (2004, pp. 114–115)

McNeill adds finer holds

McNeill (2005, pp. 31–34):

Phase What it is
preparation hand moves to where the stroke will happen
prestroke hold a brief stop before the stroke (optional)
stroke the meaningful phase — the only obligatory one
stroke hold a static stroke: holding still is the meaning
poststroke hold hand freezes after the stroke (optional)
retraction return to rest (optional) — Kendon’s recovery

The tree gesture unfolds in time

McNeill follows the same tree-bending gesture across speech:

Speech landmark What the hand does
the tree is introduced rises into position: preparation
the pronoun before the bending description briefly waits: prestroke hold
bending is described grips and pulls: stroke
the final word finishes remains in place: poststroke hold

McNeill (2005, pp. 30–33)

Paraphrase of Fig. 2.2 and the accompanying description. The actual landmarks are preparation with oak tree and, prestroke hold with he, stroke with bends it way back, and poststroke hold during the final part of back. There is no retraction here: another gesture follows immediately, so the unit continues beyond the depicted phrase. Do not add invented millisecond values. The case is described again within the assigned pp. 31–34.

Still hands: pause or meaning?

McNeill asks us to imagine a hand raised and held high to indicate the upper floor of a building.

Is the stillness just waiting — or is it the meaningful action?

Compare it with the tree gesture’s brief wait before bending.

McNeill (2005, pp. 32–33)

The upper-floor illustration is McNeill’s hypothetical example of a static stroke, not part of the observed tree gesture. Demonstrate the contrast. Expected: a stroke hold carries the meaning in the posture itself; a prestroke hold depends on an upcoming stroke. Reveal the terminology on the next slide.

A hold is not always a hold

  • A stroke hold is a stroke — the meaning is in the static posture
  • Prestroke and poststroke holds are pauses around a stroke
  • McNeill argues these holds keep the stroke synchronized with its co-expressive speech

That is McNeill’s explanation. We can observe the timing — not the speaker’s thinking.

Common traps

  • Counting one unit as one phrase
  • Putting the recovery inside the gesture phrase
  • Inventing a hold because one “should” be there
  • Splitting a gesture just because speech pauses
  • Equating a gesture phrase with a speech segment, intonation unit, or TCU
  • Calling the stroke “the fastest bit” — it is the most expressive bit
