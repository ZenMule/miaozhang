What just happened?

  • Muted: you could see that something was represented — rarely what
  • Audio only: you knew the topic, but some details were simply absent
  • Together: the speech made the movement specific; the movement added what the words left open
  • And the movement limited what the speech could plausibly mean

Kendon (2004, pp. 160–161): gestures draw on very general repertoires (pointing, shaping, acting out) that the accompanying speech makes specific.

Kendon’s continuum

McNeill (2005) arranges these along Kendon’s continuum:

gesticulation pantomime emblems sign language
hands move while speaking acting out, without speech conventional signs (👍, OK) full languages

Moving from left to right:

  • the need for speech to be present decreases
  • conventionality and language-like properties increase

This module focuses on the left end: gesticulation.

What makes gesticulation special?

McNeill (2005) describes gesticulation as:

  • co-occurring with speech — speech is obligatorily present
  • not conventionalized — no dictionary of correct forms
  • global — the parts get their meaning from the whole
  • synthetic — one gesture bundles meanings that speech spreads over several words

Speech, by contrast, is segmented and analytic: words combine upward into meanings.

Global and synthetic: an example

Retelling a cartoon, a speaker says a character bends it way back — one hand, curled as if gripping something thick, pulls backward.

  • Why curled? It is the character’s hand gripping a tree.
  • Why one hand? The tree is fixed at one end — the words never say so.

The parts make sense only through the whole image.

Speech and gesture are co-expressive but not redundant.

McNeill (2005), pp. 10–11 and pp. 39–40. Paraphrase only; do not reproduce figures. Ask students: what would a “dictionary” entry for this hand shape look like? (Impossible — that’s the point of “global”.)

Referential and pragmatic functions

Kendon (2004, pp. 158–159):

Function What the action contributes
referential content: page-turning depicts browsing the samples
pragmatic how the words are to be treated: the finger highlights a name

The action’s role depends on the words and context, not handshape alone.

Three pragmatic functions

Function What it does Example
modal changes the frame for interpreting what is said Enzo’s raised, spread hands present his claim as undeniable
performative shows the speech act or interactional move an open palm can present a proposal as something offered
parsing marks discourse parts or prominence GB’s raised finger singles out the school name

Parsing here means dividing discourse into parts — not only syntactic analysis.

Kendon (2004, pp. 158–159)

The Enzo and GB cases are summarized in the assigned extract. The open-palm proposal is Kendon’s general illustration, not a new filmed case. Keep the claim contextual: none of these forms has one fixed function everywhere.

And interaction itself

Kendon also mentions interactive or interpersonal functions — without treating them systematically:

  • showing who the utterance is addressed to
  • holding the floor while not speaking
  • regulating turns: raising a hand to ask for one, pointing to give one

Where have you seen these before in CA?

Referential or pragmatic?

“There are two problems with this plan.” — two fingers raised

“I’m not saying it’s wrong…” — hands rise, palms out, toward the listener

“First, the cost. Second, the time.” — one hand chops down on first and again on second

What is the hand contributing each time? Could any of them do more than one job?

No single right answer. Kendon treats a raised two-finger action giving prominence to “two things” as part of referential content; students may also argue for parsing. The point is to justify with the words and the context. The third case leans toward parsing. The second can be argued as modal.

No body-language dictionary

  • A palm-up hand is not always an offer
  • An index finger is not always pointing
  • A movement does not carry one fixed meaning everywhere

A label does not explain the utterance. Show what the visible action contributes — here, in these words, at this moment.
